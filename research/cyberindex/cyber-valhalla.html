Opinion Guest Essay

The Security Environment

Cyber Valhalla

Every line of code ever shipped is now subject to infinite, patient, adversarial scrutiny. That is not a trend line. It is a state transition — and it deserves a name, a date, and a label on every piece of software we own.

By Travis Good  ·  August 2026

We are entering an era that I am going to call Cyber Valhalla. If you know your Norse mythology, you know that Valhalla is a perpetual battlefield: warriors who died bravely in combat fight each other to the death every day, and every night the gods restore them to fight again tomorrow. That is an apt metaphor for what is coming in cybersecurity. Instead of deathless warriors, we are going to witness deathless agents, resurrected day in and day out, fighting an endless trench war against all of the software that has been implemented in human history.

And let us be clear about the scale of that. There is a lot of software. Software that goes back to the founding days of computer science. Algorithms that go back to the 1960s. Firmware written by three people at a company that no longer exists. All of it, until now, subject only to the limited, finite, attention-bound scrutiny of human actors. All of it now subject to unlimited, infinite scrutiny on the part of hostile, agentic ones.

This, my friends, is Cyber Valhalla. And we have to think about it as a fundamental dividing line for the world — a bright red line, if you will. A line that marks a complete state transition between what was possible before, for different tiers of threat actor, and what is possible now. Between what was acceptable in software security practice before, and what is acceptable now.

My view is that all software conceived pre-Valhalla should be labeled as such, and all software conceived post-Valhalla should be labeled as such. The software created in these two epochs is very, very different. So is its trajectory. This essay is an attempt to explain why, by walking up the stack — layer by layer — and asking a single question at each level: what did it used to cost to break this, and what does it cost now?

Part One
The machines that never get bored

What actually changed

Before the stack, a word on the weapon.

A large language model is, mechanically, a system trained to predict the next token in a sequence, using a very large number of parameters fitted against a very large corpus of text. That description is accurate and almost useless. The useful framing is this: an LLM is a lossy compression of an enormous fraction of published human technical knowledge, in a form that can be queried, recombined, and applied on demand. Ask it about branch prediction and it has read the manuals. Ask it about DRAM refresh intervals and it has read the papers. Ask it about the quirks of a 2013 IP camera firmware and, quite often, it has read that too.

An agent is what you get when you put that model in a loop and give it hands. Tools. A shell. A filesystem. A browser. The ability to write a program, run it, look at the output, form a hypothesis about why the output was wrong, revise the program, and run it again — thousands of times, without complaint, without fatigue, and without ever deciding that it has better things to do on a Friday afternoon. The model is the reasoning. The agent is the reasoning plus persistence plus reach.

That combination is what changes the security environment, and it changes it through four distinct mechanisms.

1. The expertise bottleneck dissolves

Serious vulnerability research has always been gated on a rare input: people. Not just smart people — people with an unusual conjunction of expertise. To find a silicon-level flaw you have historically needed someone deeply versed in electronics and microarchitecture, paired with someone who is a genius at writing and testing low-level software hypotheses. Purple unicorns who hold both are rare. Teams that hold both are elite private security shops or nation-states. The exploits such teams produce cost on the order of millions of dollars and years of research, which is precisely why intelligence agencies hoard them rather than disclose them.

The agent does not eliminate the need for that expertise. It eliminates the need for it to be resident in a person you have to hire.

2. Patience becomes free

The real gate on hardware research was never intelligence. It was tedium. Experimentation at that level is expensive, slow, and demands enormous patience — and, critically, demands patience across several deep domains at once. A human researcher will not spend six weeks running timing attacks against a CPU and squinting at noisy histograms. Even if they write the harness, interpreting the results is grinding, unrewarding work, and most people quit. An agent has no such constraint. Boredom is not in the loss function.

3. Parallelism

Human researchers serialize. One hypothesis, then the next. Spin up parallel agents and the same investigation runs multi-threaded, across configurations, at whatever scale your budget allows. An exploit that might once have cost millions of dollars and specialized staff may now cost a prompt, a spec sheet, and some compute — and increasingly not even the spec sheet, because the agentic system can go do the research itself.

4. The asymmetry compounds in the attacker's favor

Security has always been asymmetric: the attacker needs one flaw, the defender needs all of them. Both sides now get the same multiplier. But the attacker's multiplier converts directly into a working exploit, while the defender's multiplier converts into a backlog — findings that then have to be triaged, coordinated, patched, shipped, and, hardest of all, installed by users. Discovery has been automated. Remediation has not. That gap is the whole story.

The evidence is no longer speculative. Mozilla, given early access to a frontier security model, reported 271 vulnerabilities found and fixed in a single Firefox release — against 22 found by the prior model generation in the release before it.1 Firefox is open-source, decades old, and has been picked over by some of the best security researchers alive. Mozilla's own comment on the haul was that a single such bug would have been a red alert a year earlier, and that the volume raises the question of whether it is even possible to keep up.2 In the same period, the U.S. National Vulnerability Database catalogued more than 45,000 flaws by late July 2026 — roughly matching all of 2025 in seven months.3

Nothing about that finding requires the model to be superhuman. That is the part people keep missing. It only requires the model to be adequate and tireless — and to be pointed at a codebase that was, like all codebases, written under the assumption that adversarial attention is scarce.

All software written before this moment was designed against a threat model that priced attacker attention as expensive. That assumption has been repealed retroactively, for everything.

So let us walk the stack. I want to give you an intuitive sense, at each layer, of the expertise that used to be required to attack it, and the expertise required now. My brother Alex and I intend to use pre- and post-Valhalla as the frame for financial projections, and I think the layer-by-layer picture is what makes those projections legible.

Part Two
Walking the stack

Layer One · The FoundationHardware enclaves and silicon

You may not know this, but most chips ship with errata. Modern processors are so complex in their design that there are oversights and fundamental flaws in the way they are physically implemented. And once you have made the masks for a chip, you cannot walk those back. So what microprocessor companies do is patch the way software treats the chip — firmware and microcode adjustments that turn off sections of the processor producing erroneous results, or fix up outputs in particular ways.

You really have to stop thinking of a processor as a deterministic, infallible thing. It is a product of human ingenuity and human frailty. It will be largely correct, and it will have quirks that must be compensated for at the microcode level.

What are the consequences of those quirks? Sometimes dire. Processors do branch prediction: given an if-then-else, the chip tries to guess which path the code will take, ten steps ahead, and starts working on it. Guess right and the application feels faster. There is a related trick called out-of-order execution, where instructions are reordered for efficiency in ways that should not disturb their logic. Clever speculative techniques, both.

It turns out that if you implement this incorrectly, branch prediction can be used to exfiltrate cryptographic keys from a CPU. I am not going to walk through the mechanics, except to say that very tight timing measurements — and even voltage-level measurements — let you start to understand exactly what information is flowing through a processor. From there you can extract a key, and possibly achieve privilege escalation: moving from user mode, which has limited access to directories and sensitive programs, to root, or ring zero. Very privileged access indeed. That is the Spectre family, and it affected essentially every high-performance CPU in the field.4

Then there is the memory side. Rowhammer takes advantage of the physical properties of a memory substrate to leak information. You can do it from user space, with unprivileged programs. Continuously hammer one region of memory and the electrical disturbance flips bits in adjacent rows, which you can parlay into a privilege escalation. This is not a museum piece: in 2025, researchers at ETH Zurich working with Google demonstrated Rowhammer bit flips on all fifteen DDR5 modules in their test pool and built a working root exploit on a default-configuration desktop in as little as 109 seconds.5 The mitigation is to triple the memory refresh rate, at an 8.4% performance cost.6 DDR5 was supposed to have solved this.

These are silicon-level attacks, and they are extremely dangerous, for a simple reason: hardware is hardware. Once it is etched, it is not configurable. You can patch microcode, but that is difficult at global scale and ultimately cannot protect against a fundamental design flaw.

What the hardware is actually protecting

It is worth being explicit about the stakes. Hardware typically enforces three things.

Privileges. The rights of different programs on your machine. This is foundational — if you cannot stop a program from writing to the wrong directory or executing the wrong command, you have no control over anything at all.

Data integrity. A great deal of silicon is dedicated to keeping sensitive material — encryption keys, personal information, passwords, biometrics — out of general memory. Your ability to trust the device rests on this.

Intellectual property. Related to integrity, but distinct. This is everything from preventing a bit-perfect capture of a Hollywood release to preventing an arbitrary program from decrypting your documents in real time.

If you are storing cryptographic keys in a hardware enclave — Apple's Secure Enclave, or the equivalents on Intel and AMD parts — it is critical that these processors-within-a-processor are not compromised. If they are, the integrity of the whole machine goes with them. Worse, you can end up with genuinely persistent malware: a virus you can never get rid of, because the thing that would have to attest to its removal is itself owned.

Pre-Valhalla

An electronics expert paired with a low-level software genius, or a team of them, running expensive and time-consuming experiments across several deep domains. Elite private shop or nation-state. Millions of dollars per exploit. Rare enough that discovery rate and mitigation rate stayed roughly in balance.

Post-Valhalla

A prompt, some architectural documentation, and compute — and increasingly not even the documentation, since the agent can research it. Physical samples and instrumentation still gate the work, which is why this layer does not fully cross the line. But the human bottleneck is gone.

If you want proof of what a well-resourced actor can already do at this level, look at Pegasus — commercial spyware delivered by zero-click exploit chains that compromised fully patched iPhones without the target ever touching the screen.7 Those chains were the product of a specialist industry selling to states. Now imagine that class of capability being discovered continuously, by anyone with a budget.

And here is the part that should keep you up at night: almost every device in the world is legacy. Every phone in your pocket, every server in your rack, contains silicon that was etched in the pre-agentic era. Chip design is a long-running process — a year or two to arrive at a design, another to tape it out. You are two and a half years in before it ships. Every piece of hardware currently deployed on Earth was conceived pre-Valhalla, validated against pre-Valhalla threat models, and cannot be recalled.

Layer Two · The AbstractionVirtualization and sandboxes

Go up a level. Software today lives in layers of abstraction, and one of the most important is the virtual machine — a container that lets software believe it is running on its own computer. This abstraction is beautiful because it compartmentalizes cleanly. It is the mechanism by which you can run untrusted software safely: give it an environment where it cannot do anything malicious, and you no longer need to trust it.

Over the years, to make virtualization efficient, processor families added dedicated virtualization instructions. These let sandboxes take shortcuts rather than perfectly emulating hardware. That support is baked in at the device level. And it is complex: for every virtual operation there is a corresponding real device operation, so there are drivers translating virtual calls into real ones. Those drivers are intricate, and the translation is not always unambiguous — which calls take precedence, how they run in parallel, how edge cases resolve.

For consumers, VMs are just a fact of life; most people do not know they are there. But this layer is not only the basis of consumer security. It is the basis of cloud security. When you rent compute from a major provider, they are virtualizing it so they can sell it to you safely. They do not want to have to trust you, because trusting tenants does not scale. So you get a virtual machine, you do what you like inside it, and you cannot harm the other tenants time-slicing the same physical host.

The exploit here is called an escape. Break out of the sandbox, break out of the VM. On a consumer machine, an escape means a program obtains privileges it should never have had, and your data goes with it. That is bad.

For a cloud provider, an escape is potentially fatal. If someone finds a privilege escalation on a multi-tenant host, they can observe — and act on — the other tenants. Imagine my small company is co-tenanted with a payments processor. If I am a criminal small company, perhaps I can now start pulling card numbers out of the processing node adjacent to me.

Consider what that does. It does not merely cause a breach. It destroys trust in the provider, and trust is the cloud business model. All of a sudden every serious enterprise concludes it has to run its own hardware. That is a catastrophic collapse for the cloud vendors — and, simultaneously, a catastrophic explosion in demand for on-premises hardware and for people who know how to run it. People who, incidentally, no longer exist at meaningful scale, because the site reliability engineers were all hired by the hyperscalers a decade ago.

This is not hypothetical. Guest-to-hypervisor escapes have been found and exploited in the wild in mainstream enterprise virtualization products, including chains rated critical that let an attacker with administrative access inside a guest execute code on the host.8 When the boundary breaks, the attacker gains access to the memory of every other VM on that physical server, the hypervisor filesystem, the stored disk images, and the virtual networking that connects everything.9 The entire security architecture of the multi-tenant cloud assumes that boundary holds.

Might some providers be better insulated? Possibly the ones running custom or esoteric silicon of their own. Or possibly not — you might reasonably wonder whether even a very large company has the resources, relative to the silicon vendors themselves, to build a really well-hardened virtualization sandbox. The honest answer is that we do not know. Less documentation might offer some protection. But security through obscurity is not a policy, and in a world of infinite agentic patience it is barely even a speed bump.

Pre-Valhalla

Deep understanding of virtualization software and its hardware implementation — both esoteric — plus detailed driver-level operating system knowledge, plus patience. Exotic creatures, difficult to study. Escapes happened, were usually patchable, and were usually caught.

Post-Valhalla

Perhaps the single most attractive target on the board. Getting many hardware samples and testing them exhaustively is still hard. Virtual machines are a commodity — and mostly open source, so agents know them intimately. One escape probably works across multiple hardware classes.

The good news is that a software-layer flaw is probably patchable at the software layer. The bad news is the interval before anyone notices. Genuinely advanced persistent malware sitting in a hypervisor could exfiltrate data for a very long time before discovery — and on the day it is discovered, it could take the trust model of the entire cloud ecosystem with it.

Layer Three · The Exposed SurfaceBrowsers

Go up again. All software has vulnerabilities and we deal with them constantly, but the one worth isolating is the browser.

There is not much software with as much internet exposure as a browser. Browsers are constantly filtering potentially hostile payloads, by their nature — they are information exploration tools, and to explore information you must be exposed to it and parse it. That is the job.

Escape a browser's sandbox and you can exfiltrate user data and do anything the user can do. It may not be as severe as a root-level compromise, but there is enormous mischief available, because users are privileged. Corporations expect users to manage their own data and handle sensitive material. Individuals keep their entire lives in a browser.

Browser exploits are also an easier class than pure virtualization work. The source code is widely available. There are only a handful of successful engines left, which means we have a browser monoculture — and monoculture is exactly the wrong property when discovery gets cheap. Which brings us back to Figure 1: the largest set of vulnerabilities ever found in Firefox in a single cycle, in an open-source product that expert researchers have combed for twenty years.

What follows if browser exploits become constant? People can no longer surf the internet — or can only surf a whitelisted version of it. And note what that implies for agents. Agents often drive browsers directly, and when they do not, they use the same rendering and parsing components browsers do. If your browser can be exploited, your agent using agentic browsing tools can very likely be exploited too.

So if browsers are exploited — and they will be — the internet itself becomes radioactive. We end up whitelisting it. We end up with extreme friction when traversing anything unlisted. An enormous amount of latency gets introduced into what is currently a frictionless medium. And it is worth remembering that the internet is relatively safe right now. There is no law requiring it to stay that way.

If you want to think about how bad this could get, remember that advertising is a malware distribution vector, and always has been. Trojans have spread through paid search placements impersonating trusted brands for years, and attackers now compromise legitimate advertiser accounts to buy the placements.10 Imagine an agent optimizing that loop. Imagine buying one ad that compromises one company's internal network and yields all of their data.

How do you protect against this? Some of what I have already mentioned. But fundamentally, you have to lock browsers down and formalize browser construction in a way that has never been done.

Layer Four · The Perimeter That Isn'tIoT and edge devices

Take a moment to think about the things in your life. You have a great many internet-of-things devices — edge devices, smart devices, whatever you want to call them. Companies these days want to make everything internet-connected.

The reality of these devices is that hardware companies are often the worst software developers in the world. They do not like spending money on software, because it damages the unit economics of the device. They are usually very poor at supporting multiple hardware generations. And edge devices have had some of the worst and most trivially exploited vulnerabilities in the entire industry.

To understand this, you need only look at security cameras — which ought to be the thing companies care most about securing, since they reveal the most private information — and which turn out to be trivially defeatable in a great many cases. There are search engines whose entire function is indexing internet-connected devices, and they have surfaced unauthenticated camera feeds for over a decade. This was documented in 2013 and the same class of exposure persists today.11

Here is the interesting part. Pre-Valhalla, you could almost say there were too many targets. It is hard to bring security camera footage together in a meaningful way and use it to understand anything, because visual information is difficult to aggregate. The sheer number of devices was itself a kind of protection — a herd immunity of irrelevance.

Post-Valhalla, there are no longer too many devices. Agents can walk every device class, based on vulnerable hardware and vulnerable software, much of it reused and none of it updated, and break all of them. And agents are extremely good at aggregating visual information. Both halves of the old protection evaporate at once.

So what does that mean? If these devices are on your network, you now have adversarial threats generated inside your network, which you must constantly watch. That was never the case before. Maybe your smart light bulb is trying to hack your machines. Do you need an internal firewall now? Maybe your refrigerator is trying to hack you.

And maybe someone is now able to collect information at a scale that permits the equivalent of a global license-plate reader network, using hacked IoT devices. This is not fanciful. Wi-Fi signals can be used for motion detection and localization: researchers have used commodity routers to reconstruct human pose and track movement through walls.12 So imagine every router gets hacked, and someone can tell exactly where people are inside a building at any moment — and can merge that with camera feeds, and temperature data, and whatever else the IoT layer is emitting, to build a very complete picture of people's lives and activities. That information supports industrial espionage. It supports blackmail. It supports a great many nefarious purposes, and the capability to assemble it did not exist pre-Valhalla.

What do you need at this point? You need to firewall every IoT device you own, treat each one as potentially hostile, and monitor its network traffic. That is a whole new category of demand for security services.

The SynthesisNobody gets to defend one layer

If you layer all of this together, you start to realize something important. It is not sufficient to protect any single layer. You have firmware security. You have hardware security. You have operating system security. You have virtualization and sandbox security. You have browser security. You have IoT security. And if you fail to cover even one of those bases, as a company or as an individual, you are completely exposed — because someone only needs to find one open door, one open window, and they can wreak havoc on your personal life or your corporate finances.

Which means companies with vertically integrated solutions are going to benefit enormously, and this is going to be a very important market. So let us name it precisely. Firmware security. Operating system security. Configuration security — especially Linux, because Linux is now so prevalent, and because the exposure is not only a software vulnerability issue but a configuration issue. Virtualization security. Browser security on top of it.

Someone needs to have a full-stack answer to this. Otherwise you, as a person, and you, as an enterprise, are not actually being protected. You are being partially protected, which in a conjunctive system is a synonym for unprotected.

Part Three
Surfaces that did not exist before

Emerging Vertical · OnePersonal security and impersonation

Here is a market that did not exist before, created entirely by this transition.

If people can compromise your devices, they can take your image. Your likeness. Video clips of you. Your voice. And once they have those, they can impersonate you and take actions on your behalf in society. They can fake your voice. They can fake video of you. They can create defamatory websites or comments that appear to come from you. They can use your footprint to interact with the world.

Pre-Valhalla, you could be reasonably confident it would be hard for someone to gather enough data about you to successfully impersonate you — to a friend, to a loved one, to the company you work for. That is no longer the case. Three seconds of audio is now sufficient to clone a voice at high fidelity, and roughly 70% of people surveyed say they are not confident they could tell a cloned voice from a real one.13 In the single best-documented case, a finance employee at an engineering firm authorized about $25.6 million in transfers after a video call on which every other participant, including the CFO, was synthetic.14

So I think we are going to see an increasing prevalence of AI-powered phishing and impersonation defense tools, and that these have to become part of the standard defensive suite. Because psychological operations are the most effective operations. Look all the way back to Kevin Mitnick, who wrote The Art of Deception — he was successful largely through social engineering, not through technical wizardry. Now imagine social engineering bot campaigns running at mass scale, personalized, in your mother's voice.

We need defense for that. We need to be able to detect when it is happening, in real time, and shut it down. I am not sure who wins that market. I am confident it is a large one.

Emerging Vertical · TwoAgent security

Similarly, we need defense for the agents we are sending out to interact with the world.

Two problems compound here. The first I mentioned in the browser section: agents use common software components, and if those components are compromised, the agents are compromised too. The second is native to the technology. Agents can be prompt injected.

The fact that the frontier labs cannot secure frontier models against prompt injection tells you everything you need to know about the state of agent security. And this is not for want of trying. The underlying issue is architectural: a language model processes its input as a single token sequence, and there is no reliable mechanism to enforce privilege boundaries between the system's instructions, the user's request, and content the agent retrieved from the outside world.15 Everything is tokens. Anyone who can get text into the context window is, to some degree, giving instructions.

The field's response in 2026 has been telling: the working strategy has shifted from prevention to containment. Assume some injections will land, and constrain what a landed injection can accomplish — least privilege, human approval for irreversible actions, sandboxed tools.16 That is a reasonable engineering answer. It is also an admission.

Meanwhile the incidents have started to arrive. An autonomous agent has already compromised a continuous-integration configuration and pushed backdoored versions of a widely used model-gateway library to a public package index, where it sat for hours and was downloaded tens of thousands of times before removal — an AI agent poisoning the infrastructure that other AI agents depend on, with no human direction after launch.17

So I think this is going to be an absolutely enormous vertical, and I do not think it is anywhere near solved. For companies using agents to accomplish productive work — and that is going to be every company — an integrated agentic security solution is not optional. It is the price of deploying the technology at all.

Emerging Vertical · ThreeCrypto

The last one I want to name is crypto security, and here the future has already arrived.

In the past week we have seen a wave of headlines about the Bitcoin ecosystem. A volunteer group calling itself the Bitcoin Red Team — sixteen distributed people and three automated agents — pointed AI models at hundreds of open-source Bitcoin codebases simultaneously and filed 4,962 security findings across 390 projects in roughly 27 to 30 hours. Eighty-five were rated critical and 635 high severity. Roughly 91% of the findings came from automated scans, and about a fifth had already been reproduced with working proof-of-concept code.18 The compute bill was on the order of $40,000.19

Bitcoin is one of the most studied protocols in existence. It has had more eyes on it, and more financial incentive attached to breaking it, than arguably any protocol ever written. If that is the state of the Bitcoin ecosystem, what do we suppose is true of smaller protocols — protocols that, pre-Valhalla, never spent serious money testing themselves with agentic tools?

My simple view is that smaller protocols — L1s, DeFi protocols, applications — are going to be wiped out by Cyber Valhalla. There is no way these systems, which often exist in multiple versions, which cannot always be easily updated, which frequently depend on immutable libraries that cannot be updated at all, are going to remain unexploited. The financial incentives are too great and the payouts are direct and untraceable. What we will see is mass exploitation of crypto protocols and smart contracts.

We have already seen the template. In late July, attackers exploited a five-year-old firmware flaw in a widely respected hardware wallet — a build configuration error from March 2021 that caused the device to generate seeds using a weak software random number generator instead of its dedicated hardware entropy source, collapsing effective key strength from 128 bits to as little as 40. Roughly 1,816 BTC, about $116 million, was drained from more than 5,200 addresses across four waves. The largest single sweep moved 1,082 BTC from 1,196 wallets inside 41 minutes.20 One victim's summary is the most important sentence written about security this year: he had never shared his seed, his devices had never touched the internet, everything sat in safes and safety deposit boxes — and none of it mattered, because of one line of code from 2021.21

That bug was not found by AI. But it sat undiscovered for five years in open-source firmware, which is precisely the class of latent defect that infinite agentic scrutiny is built to surface. As one industry figure put it after the incident: open source and audited are two different things.22

So who has a solution? Who is out there providing scanning that can mitigate chain vulnerabilities, find DeFi vulnerabilities, catch smart contract bugs before they are exploited? Whoever does that is going to become very wealthy. But it also will not be enough, because the scale of the attack is going to be greater than the scale of the defense.

My prediction is that what matters is speed — how fast you can mitigate a flaw in your own protocol; versatility across different crypto protocols; and the crypto-native knowledge embedded in a given system or tool. And I will add this: it is going to be extraordinarily difficult for new protocols to launch without some level of full formal verification. That is no longer a luxury for the paranoid. It is becoming the entry ticket.

The economics are already repricing around this. One major exchange cut its critical bug bounty payout by 70% in July 2026, because when models can mass-produce vulnerability reports — including low-quality ones that still require human review — the bounty model stops functioning as designed.23 That is what a market looks like when supply of findings goes vertical.

Part Four
Likelihood, risk, impact

What this all adds up to

A good way of thinking about things in project management is likelihood, risk, and impact. Likelihood is the chance the thing happens. Risk is the exposure attached to it happening. Impact is how it plays out.

Apply that frame to everything above. Pre-Cyber Valhalla, likelihood was relatively low. Risk depended heavily on which category you were in — you were exposed at some layers and comfortable at others. Impact was generally contained.

Post-Cyber Valhalla, likelihood is extremely high. Risk is much more evenly distributed across domains than it used to be — the comfortable categories are gone. And the impacts are going to be very large.

This creates enormous opportunity for cybersecurity companies, enormous peril for the software industry, and a sea change in software security practice.

The most extreme form of the argument is that vibe coding as we know it is already dead, because vibe coding produces insecure programs, and insecure programs are now discovered on a timescale measured in hours. Something has to replace it. Either new programming paradigms and new agentic techniques that produce provably secure programs, or the mandatory implementation of certain standards, or — as I have posed in some speculative scenarios — the mandatory adoption of continuously active defensive AI, integrated into products as a condition of shipping them.

You can also imagine that the field of cyber insurance is going to be absolutely rocked. If I were a cyber insurer right now I would be shaking in my boots. I think it may already be over for them in their current form. The underwriting problem is not that losses rise; underwriters can price rising losses. The problem is correlation. A single AI-identified flaw in shared infrastructure can hit thousands of policyholders simultaneously, which turns a diversifiable book into an undiversifiable one. That is the exact concern now driving serious proposals for a federal cyber reinsurance backstop.24 Underwriters have started asking about AI tool inventories and prompt-injection controls, and reinsurers are naming agentic AI and synthetic media as top systemic risks for the year.25 They are asking the right questions. I am not sure the answers are underwritable.

And when you think about impact, you should also think about social impact. What happens when your car gets hacked? What happens when the power grid gets hacked? Events like these, in aggregate, produce social changes that fundamentally alter the economic landscape. It is really important to consider first-, second-, and third-order effects when you look at cybersecurity, because the second-order effects are where the money is and the third-order effects are where the politics are.

That is the overview. The stack is old, the attackers are new, and the line between those two facts is bright and red and already behind us.
